Chapter 3. Hedging and stance

NoteObjectives
  • Recognise hedges, boosters and other stance markers in scientific texts.
  • Calibrate the strength of claims to the strength of the evidence.
  • Avoid both overclaiming and excessive hedging.

Required reading

Hyland, K. (1996). Writing without conviction? Hedging in science research articles. Applied Linguistics, 17(4), 433-454.

1. Why researchers hedge

Hyland (1996) analysed hedging in research articles in cell and molecular biology and showed that it is not a sign of weakness but a central feature of scientific argument. Hedges (may, might, suggest, possibly, appear to, likely) do two jobs: they state claims accurately, matching them to the evidence, and they show respect for the reader and the community, leaving room for disagreement. Boosters (clearly, demonstrate, undoubtedly, it is well known that) do the opposite: they express certainty.

Good scientific writing calibrates: strong evidence, strong verbs; weak or indirect evidence, cautious verbs.

Strength Verbs and expressions Use when
Strong show, demonstrate, establish, confirm Robust, replicated evidence, ideally causal
Moderate indicate, suggest, support Good evidence with limits (observational, single study)
Tentative may, might, could, possibly, it is possible that Speculation, interpretation, future directions

2. Overclaiming and spin

Overclaiming (saying more than the data allow) is common in abstracts and press releases: causal language for correlational data (“X causes Y” instead of “X is associated with Y”), generalising from a sample to a population, or calling a non-significant result “a trend towards significance”. Reviewers and readers notice, and it damages credibility.

But excessive hedging is also a problem, especially for non-native writers who learn that caution is safe: “It might possibly be suggested that the results could perhaps indicate…” says nothing. One hedge per claim is usually enough.

3. Reporting other people’s work

Reporting verbs carry your stance towards a source:

  • Neutral: Smith (2020) reports / describes / finds that…
  • Positive: Smith (2020) shows / demonstrates / establishes that… (you accept the claim)
  • Critical or distant: Smith (2020) claims / asserts / argues that… (you leave room for doubt)

{

  const boosters = ["clearly", "demonstrate", "demonstrates", "undoubtedly", "certainly", "prove", "proves", "obviously", "definitely", "always", "never", "it is well known", "establish", "establishes", "show that", "shows that", "causes", "cause"];
  const esc = s => s.replace(/&/g, "&amp;").replace(/</g, "&lt;");

}

  1. Analyse the discussion section of one of your papers or drafts with the lab.
  2. For each main claim, describe the evidence that supports it (design, sample, replication) and decide whether the verb is too strong, too weak or right.
  3. Rewrite the paragraph with calibrated claims and explain your changes.
