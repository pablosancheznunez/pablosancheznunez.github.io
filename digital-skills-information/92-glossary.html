Appendix E — Glossary

Terms in the book, ordered by chapter. Each entry links to the chapter where it is explained.

Information sources

Information overload

A situation in which you receive more information than you can process, with effects such as anxiety or feeling less well informed. → Unit 1

Disinformation

False information created or spread with the intention to deceive. → Unit 1

Misinformation

False information shared without the intention to deceive. → Unit 1

Credibility

The degree to which a source or a message is perceived as trustworthy. Online it is often judged with mental shortcuts (heuristics) such as reputation or endorsement by others. → Unit 1

Lateral reading

A verification strategy that consists of leaving the page and consulting other sources about it and about what it claims, instead of analysing it only from the inside. → Unit 1

SIFT method

Mike Caulfield’s method for verifying information in four steps: stop, investigate the source, find better coverage and trace claims back to the original. → Unit 1

Primary source

A source containing original information: a research article, a dataset, a law or an interview. Secondary sources analyse or summarise it; tertiary sources help you find it. → Unit 1

Peer review

Evaluation of a piece of work by specialists in the field before it is published. → Unit 1

Altmetrics

Measurement of mentions of scientific publications on social media, in the news and in other online sources. It measures attention, not quality. → Unit 1

Information retrieval

Information need

What a person wants to know and why; it is the starting point of every search. → Unit 2

Boolean operator

A word that combines search terms as sets: AND requires all of them, OR accepts any of them and NOT excludes. → Unit 2

Truncation

The use of a symbol, usually the asterisk, to retrieve all the endings of a stem. → Unit 2

Field searching

A search limited to one part of the record, such as the title, the author or the abstract. → Unit 2

Precision

The proportion of retrieved results that are relevant. → Unit 2

Recall

The proportion of all relevant documents that the search retrieves. → Unit 2

Berrypicking

Marcia Bates’s model in which searching is a process that changes as you read, picking up useful information at each step. → Unit 2

Controlled vocabulary

A set of standardised terms (thesaurus, subject headings) that a database uses to describe documents and group synonyms. → Unit 2

Ethical use of information and plagiarism

Intellectual property

The set of authors’ rights over their original creations: moral rights (which cannot be waived) and economic rights. → Unit 3

Right of quotation

A limit to intellectual property that allows fragments of other people’s works to be included for analysis or commentary for teaching or research purposes, stating the source and the author. → Unit 3

Public domain

The status of a work whose economic rights have expired and which can be used freely, respecting moral rights. → Unit 3

Creative Commons licence

A licence with which the author authorises certain uses of their work in advance, combining the conditions BY, SA, NC and ND. → Unit 3

Plagiarism

Presenting someone else’s work, ideas or words as your own without acknowledging them. → Unit 3

Paraphrase

Expressing an author’s ideas in your own words and structure, accompanied by a citation. → Unit 3

Patchwriting

Copying a text while changing some words or their order, but keeping its structure. It is a form of plagiarism. → Unit 3

Academic integrity

A commitment to honesty, trust, fairness, respect and responsibility in study and research. → Unit 3

References

Citation

A short marker in the text that points to the source used, such as (Bates, 1989) or [3]. → Units 4 and 5

Reference

The full description of a source in the final list of a piece of work. → Units 4 and 5

Citation style

A set of rules establishing which details citations and references include, in what order and with what punctuation (APA, Vancouver, ISO 690…). → Units 4 and 5

Author-date system

A citation system that identifies the source in the text by surname and year, and orders the list alphabetically. → Units 4 and 5

Numeric system

A citation system that identifies the source with a number and orders the list by order of appearance. → Units 4 and 5

DOI

Digital Object Identifier. A permanent identifier for a digital document that resolves at https://doi.org/. → Units 4 and 5

Reference manager

Software that stores and organises references, inserts citations into the word processor and generates the bibliography in a style. → Units 4 and 5

Similarity-checking tool

Software that compares a text with large collections of documents and flags overlaps. Its report is an indication, not a verdict of plagiarism. → Units 4 and 5

Generative artificial intelligence

Generative artificial intelligence

AI that produces new content (text, images, audio, code) from patterns learned from large amounts of data. → Units 6 and 7

Language model

A system trained on large collections of texts to predict the most likely fragment that follows a given text. → Units 6 and 7

Prompt

The text with which you ask a generative AI system for something. → Units 6 and 7

Hallucination

An AI answer that looks correct but is false or invented, such as a non-existent reference. → Units 6 and 7

Training data

The collection of data with which a model is trained; it determines what the model knows and the biases it reproduces. → Units 6 and 7

Retrieval-augmented generation

RAG. A technique that combines a language model with a search engine or document collection to answer from what is retrieved. → Units 6 and 7

Algorithmic bias

A systematic error in an automated system that favours or disadvantages certain groups, often inherited from the data. → Units 8 and 9

AI use statement

A text stating which AI tool was used in a piece of work, what for, in which parts and how the result was checked. → Units 8 and 9

AI Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, which regulates AI in the European Union with a risk-based approach. → Units 8 and 9

AI literacy

The knowledge and skills needed to use AI in an informed way and understand its opportunities, risks and possible harms. → Units 8 and 9

Explicability

An ethical principle according to which it must be possible to understand how an AI system works and who is accountable for it. → Units 8 and 9

Personal data

Any information about an identified or identifiable natural person. → Units 8 and 9

Informational self-determination

Each person’s right to decide on the use of their personal data. → Units 8 and 9
